# The owner's decisions on topics, examples and messages (input of the topic stage)

Curated 2026-11-25 from the owner's answers on approval cards and in the threads. It is the ground truth about what the owner accepts and rejects. The topic stage (`topic_selector.md`, Fable) reads it in full before choosing, and learns the PATTERNS from it, never the cases: a new example must not be a re-skin of an approved or rejected one. Hebrew sentences are quoted as they were shown to the owner.

Evidence levels: **approved** and **rejected** are the owner's own decisions. **pending** means proposed and not yet decided: use it only to avoid repeating the example, never as evidence of taste. "Reason" is the owner's own words, paraphrased; "(inferred)" marks a reason the owner did not state and that was read from the replacement they asked for.

Update rule (standing): whenever the owner approves or rejects a topic, example or message, or comments on one in a finished video, append the case here in the same format (a PR to `dev`), and add a pattern to sections B-C only if it repeats.

## A. Cases

**0.0 Course trailer. APPROVED.** Example: between any two Facebook users there are on average 3.57 people (a measured fact about a network). The message first said discrete math is "the foundational language of computer science". Owner: loved the example ("sounds interesting"), but asked to check that the claim is right and consistent with the sources. Result: "language" overclaimed (the sources present discrete math as a branch of mathematics that CS builds on), so the message became "the foundational tool of computer science". Lesson: the example may be a surprising real fact; the framing must not overclaim.

**1.0 "Is dad lying?" (if-then promise).**
- Bike promise ("if you get 100 I buy you a bike"; got 70 and got the bike). Owner: good idea, but the violation case is not logical: if you get a bike either way, both outcomes sound fine. REJECTED as built.
- Reversed ("if you fail you won't get a phone"). REJECTED (the owner asked again for a search with a clearer proposal).
- Coffee prices promise by a politician. Owner: liked the election promise, but wanted something more relevant and more interesting than coffee, and a politician. REJECTED as built, direction kept.
- Student rent promise by an imaginary politician. Replaced by the owner's own wording.
- **APPROVED (owner's own formulation):** a politician promised "if elected, I will lower taxes". He lost, and taxes went up or down: it looks as if he lied, yet he kept the promise, because it only applies if he is elected. Reason: the violation (elected and did not lower taxes) is clearly wrong, and the case that only looks like a violation is a real riddle with a twist.
- An alternative offered later (the one-million-dollar challenge for a supernatural power: a thousand tried, nobody passed, so the promise was not broken). The owner did not take it; the taxes version stayed.

**1.1 Proposition and truth value.**
- WhatsApp group chat (question, greeting, request vs claims). REJECTED: too obvious ("everyone knows a question is not a proposition"; do not teach the obvious).
- Restaurant bill split, quiz grades, age on an ID, supermarket receipt. REJECTED: still clumsy, a scene with a calculation instead of one sentence.
- "He is 19" (who is "he"?). REJECTED: a trick on the definition itself, confusing.
- **APPROVED: the Dead Sea.** "The Dead Sea is the lowest place on land" (true), "the Dead Sea is the saltiest water in the world" (false: a small pond in Antarctica is saltier). Why (the owner asked for the explanation so it generalises): one sentence, a subject everyone knows, the surprise comes from the world and not from the definition, the surprise IS the message (a false sentence is still a proposition), a clean pair on one subject, a reliable source, short natural Hebrew, a bridge to math with the same structure. Owner's framing: not too easy, not too hard, the message must pass clearly, in good Hebrew, for the right audience.

**1.2 Negation. APPROVED.** A match that ended 1:1: "Maccabi won" (false), "Maccabi lost" (false), "Maccabi did not win" (true). The negation of p is "it is not true that p", not the opposite of p. Bridge: 1>1, 1<1.

**1.3 "And" (conjunction).** The owner rejected five candidates before approving one.
- The Great Wall ("longer than 20,000 km and visible from the Moon", a myth). REJECTED.
- Cyprus ("in the EU, but drives on the left"). REJECTED: the word "but" is not the word being taught ("why bring in but? use and, that is the sentence").
- Syllabus pass rule (final 60+ and exam 55+). REJECTED: a dry rule with a scenario to compute (inferred).
- The Israeli sabra cactus (national symbol and a native plant). REJECTED: "does not speak enough to the audience".
- Coffee ("stimulates, but dehydrates"). REJECTED: again "but".
- Waze and Iron Dome / cherry tomato. Cherry tomato dropped; the owner then asked for two interesting RELATED facts with a visible link.
- **APPROVED: Waze.** "Waze was invented in Israel, and Google bought it" (true and true: T and T) against "...and Google founded it" (false). Why: two true facts about one story with a visible connection, relevant to Israeli students (an app they use), the pure word "and", and a one-word swap makes the false variant. The owner also asked "are you sure it is false?": every claim needs a source and wording whose false part cannot be disputed.

**1.4 "Or" (inclusive and exclusive). APPROVED** (twice, the second on a re-approval card). "Egypt is in Africa or in Asia" is true even though both parts are true (Sinai), and "exclusive or" turns it false. A riddle: the everyday "or" means "one of them", logic says both is fine.

**1.5 What is a logical connective.**
- "July is hot because the Earth is farthest from the Sun" vs "...because the sun's rays warm" (two true parts, the "because" decides the truth). The owner liked the sun-rays idea ("cute", and more logical when the true sentence is "because the rays warm") and asked to look for something better. The proposer judged that the false reason was too absurd, nobody would err on it. Not approved.
- **APPROVED: daylight saving time.** "There is more light in summer because we move the clock" is false; "we move the clock because there is more light in summer" is true; the same two true parts. So "because" is not a connective (the truth is not decided by the values of the two parts alone). Why (inferred): both sentences sound plausible, swapping the same two parts flips the truth, it is an everyday thing.
- Also learned here: the owner did not know what a logical connective is and could decide only after a plain explanation. A topic whose point is not obvious to a smart non-expert needs a one-sentence plain explanation inside the message itself.
- The owner questioned the position of 1.5 in the order (he would put it after all the connectives). Topic order belongs to the curriculum, not to this stage.

**1.6 If-then and a promise not broken. APPROVED with the owner's instruction:** reuse the election promise of 1.0 and go into detail (four cases: elected and lowered taxes; elected and did not lower them, the only violation; not elected, taxes went up or down: the promise was not tested). The boiling water in Jerusalem (97 degrees) example was liked as "a cute example" and set aside for another video. Why: one familiar story that grows from video to video lets the viewer spend all the attention on the new concept. The owner added: keep the creativity and the interest.

**1.7 Vacuous truth. APPROVED.** "All of Israel's medals at the Winter Olympics are gold" is true because there are no medals at all (also "all are bronze"). The owner liked the claim and asked how vacuous truth can work at all; the one-sentence answer ("it is the same if-then: it is true whenever the condition never happens") belongs in the video. Why: a funny, checkable, absurd-looking claim that is logically true.

**1.9 Precedence and brackets. APPROVED.** The viral argument over 8÷2(2+2): both sides read by a rule, the result 1 or 16, no brackets. Bridge to p∨q∧r. Why: students know the argument, and it shows that an agreed convention decides.

**1.11 Necessary and sufficient. APPROVED.** The blue tick in WhatsApp: it appears only if the person read the message (necessary for "read"), but no tick does not mean "did not read" (read receipts can be switched off). Why: an app every student uses, and both directions are visible on screen.

**1.8 "If and only if" (↔).** Blood-type donation (O+ vs O-). REJECTED (reason not stated). iPhone yellow battery ("yellow if and only if power saving is on"). REJECTED (reason not stated). Saturn's rings ("a planet has rings if and only if it is Saturn", false; Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune have rings too; "...one of the four giants" is true). PENDING.

**1.10 Formalisation.** Pluto ("why is Pluto not a planet": three conditions joined by and). REJECTED (reason not stated). Exam entry "only if you submitted 80% of the exercises" (Noa submitted all and did not enter: no violation; Yoni did not submit and entered: violation). PENDING.

**1.12 Building a truth table (2^n rows).** A falafel stand with 8 toppings and 256 pitas. REJECTED: "not clear how the answer is 256" (inferred: too big to see or draw). A pita with 4 toppings (guess 8, answer 16; show 1 topping gives 2, two give 4, three give 8, four give 16). PENDING (the owner asked for exactly this smaller version).

**1.13 Truth table of a compound sentence.** The 29th of February in 2100 (a rule with and/or/not). REJECTED: too far from the student's life (inferred from the replacement requested). An exam entry rule "(student card or ID) and not late". PENDING.

**1.10 Formalization (הצרנה).** Library sign "אין להכניס אוכל ושתייה" (with "and"). CHANGED BY THE OWNER (2026-11-26): use "אין להכניס אוכל או שתייה" (with "or"): a far more logical, relevant and easy-to-understand real-life wording. Pattern: prefer the wording people really see on a sign over a constructed one; the pizza-only case stays the deciding case (word-by-word ¬p∨¬q allows it, the meaning ¬p∧¬q forbids it).

## B. Patterns that worked (approved) and why

1. **A familiar subject, one sentence.** Dead Sea, Waze, daylight saving, the viral 8÷2(2+2) argument, the WhatsApp blue tick, Facebook's 3.57. Nothing to set up; the sentence itself is the example.
2. **The surprise comes from the world, and the surprise is the message.** The student's confident first answer is wrong (saltiest sea, an elected politician "lied", Sinai, a clock "creating" daylight) and the correction is exactly the concept. Never a trick on the definition (rejected "he is 19").
3. **A clean pair on one subject** where one detail flips the truth (a true/false pair, one swapped word or one swapped direction). The contrast is seen, not explained.
4. **Two related true facts with a visible link** (Waze invented in Israel + bought by Google), not two unrelated trivia.
5. **A riddle with a beat of tension:** ask, hold a real pause, answer. The twist is the message.
6. **The taught word itself, pure.** "And" without "but", "or" as it is, no neighbouring concept in the sentence.
7. **A rule or promise whose violation feels clearly wrong,** while the case that only looks like a violation is the riddle. If the outcome sounds acceptable both ways there is no riddle.
8. **Relevant to an Israeli student of 18 to 25 this week:** apps (Waze, WhatsApp, iPhone), politics and taxes, exams and campus, football, the news, a myth they were taught at school. Slightly challenging, never obvious, never remote.
9. **Small enough to see.** Four toppings you can draw beat 256 you must compute. Numbers in the example stay tiny; the pattern is shown before a big number is hinted.
10. **One story can carry two neighbouring videos** (1.0 and 1.6 share the election promise) when the new concept builds directly on the old one. Use it deliberately, not by default.
11. **Checkable and carefully worded.** Every fact has a reliable source, and the wording is the weakest claim that keeps the point ("invented", not "developed", when the false part is the invention). When the owner asks "are you sure?", the source answers.
12. **Creative and interesting beats merely correct.** The owner praised cute, surprising choices (the Jerusalem boiling point, the Winter Olympics medals) and asked for more of that.

## C. Patterns that failed (rejected) and why

1. **The obvious** (WhatsApp question vs claim): the student already knows the point, so nothing is learned.
2. **A scene with a calculation** (bill, grades, receipt, syllabus rule): clumsy; the viewer works instead of seeing the idea.
3. **A trick on the definition** ("who is he?"): confuses and teaches nothing about the concept.
4. **A violation that sounds fine either way** (the bike either way): the promise logic is lost.
5. **Dry or distant:** a rule list, a remote myth, a national symbol that does not speak to this audience (sabra), a medical table (blood types, reason not stated).
6. **A neighbouring word in the sentence** ("but", "because") when another concept is being taught.
7. **Too big to picture** (256 pitas, a date rule over a century): the example hides the pattern.
8. **Overclaiming** ("language of computer science").
9. **An absurd false variant** (nobody would believe the false sentence): no tension, so no learning.
10. **Something the owner cannot explain to himself.** If a smart non-expert cannot say in one sentence what the concept is after reading the message, the message or the example is unclear.

## D. How the owner decides (to predict the decision)
He judges in seconds, from one sentence and a message of 14 words or fewer. He reads for: do I get it at once, is it interesting, does it speak to students, is the logic honest, is the Hebrew natural. He reacts to the concept's meaning first (he asks what a logical connective is, how vacuous truth can be true). He likes a version that keeps working across videos, and rewards creativity.
